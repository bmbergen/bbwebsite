Porto

Porto announces itself the moment you step off the train: São Bento Station's entrance hall is wrapped floor to ceiling in blue-and-white azulejo tiles depicting scenes from Portuguese history, a fitting backdrop for watching generations pass through on their way somewhere else. Just outside, the city's tilework habit continues — the deep-blue facade of Capela das Almas, a jacaranda blooming purple against another patterned building nearby, and one more ornately tiled townhouse a few streets over. Up near the University of Porto, the Fonte dos Leões sends water arcing past the Baroque front of Igreja do Carmo, while the Ribeira waterfront below hums with cafes and colorful rowhouses stacked along the Douro. The iron double-deck Dom Luís I Bridge carries foot traffic across to Vila Nova de Gaia, where a cable car climbs to Jardim do Morro for a postcard view back over the river, and where the real business of Porto waits in cool cellar rooms — a Sandeman port barrel stamped with the label's silhouetted Don, and a flight of tawny, ruby, and white poured for tasting at the end of the day.
